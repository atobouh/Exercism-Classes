VLAN trunks

Page 4 of 6 in this chapter. Your notes on this page: 1.

An access port carries one VLAN. A trunk carries all of them on the same cable, so every frame needs a label saying which VLAN it belongs to.

So a trunk is like one road with lanes painted on it. The tag is the lane.
Your note, yesterday

That label is the 802.1Q tag: four bytes the switch slips into the frame, holding a twelve-bit VLAN number. The switch at the far end reads the tag, takes it out again, and delivers the frame only inside that VLAN.

The frame from Sales gets its tag where it enters the trunk.

One VLAN is allowed to cross untagged: the native VLAN, VLAN 1 unless you change it. Both ends must agree on which VLAN that is, or frames quietly land in the wrong one.

The native VLAN is 1. How does a frame from PC-SALES cross the trunk?

Next you will give each VLAN a door into the router, on one physical port. That is router-on-a-stick, and it is the lab at the end of this chapter.

The books

Each course is a cloth-bound book with its own cover. Ownership is the point: it sits on your shelf, the ribbon shows where you stopped, and nothing counts points.

The page

Course text is set like a printed book. Your own words are in green, in the app's sans, so you can always tell the book from you. Focus mode dims everything but the paragraph you are on, the way iA Writer does.

Collections

Select any sentence, command or diagram and collect it, the way Are.na works. Over months they become your own reference: your commands, your mistakes, your diagrams.

Practice stays inside

The live diagram and the question are part of the page. Review keeps the plain card you already liked.

The lab

Opened from the chapter, it takes the whole window: a calm map of your network you can drag, add to and connect, like UniFi on an Obsidian canvas. Each device has its console, its ports, and a Zed-style diff of what your commands changed. A failed check marks the spot where traffic stops.
